## Words and phrases to strike

### Group 1 — the plain-monosyllable "earned wisdom" register (the live 2026 layer)
All from Cook, May 2026, with her flagged collocations. Individually ordinary words; the tell is the
collocation and the density.

quietly ("quietly building/dominating/transforming") · shift ("a shift in thinking", "the shift towards") ·
matters ("this matters because", "what matters here") · shape ("shapes the future", "shapes how we think") ·
land (a point or idea that "lands") · actually (bare filler intensifier) · real ("real growth", "the real
reason", "real value", "real impact") · earn ("earn the right to", "earn trust", "earn attention") ·
the work ("do the work", "trust the work", "the work itself") · hold ("hold space", "hold the line") ·
pull ("the pull of", "feel the pull towards") · compound ("decisions compound", "small actions compound") ·
signal ("send the signal", "a strong signal") · built different ("built to last", "built for the bold")

Also **stark** — named as newly frequent (Gregory Agency).

### Group 2 — Latinate verbs and adverbs (older layer, still statistically elevated)
Matsui's highest-increase terms: delve, underscore, primarily, meticulous, boast, commendable, showcase,
surpass, intricate, tapestry, unlocking. Vollmer adds: pivotal, realm, aligns, underpins, garnered, bolstering.

Two independent sources agree on **boast, commendable, surpass, primarily** — less well known than "delve"
and worth an explicit check.

### Group 3 — metaphor nouns (Vollmer)
tapestry, landscape, realm, mosaic, ecosystem, symphony, labyrinth, beacon, cornerstone, bedrock, testament.
(Note: "Bedrock" as the AWS product name is not a hit.)

### Group 4 — empty evaluative adjectives (Vollmer)
robust, seamless, pivotal, vibrant, dynamic, comprehensive, multifaceted, nuanced, holistic.

### Group 5 — signposting phrases (Vollmer)
"It's important to note that…" · "It's worth noting that…" · "When it comes to…" · "In the realm of…" ·
"At its core…"

### Group 6 — fake-conversational hooks (Gregory Agency)
"Here's the thing:" · "Think about it:" · "There's a catch:" · "In the ever-evolving world of…"
(the agency calls the last one the single biggest tell)

### Group 7 — closing rituals (Vollmer)
"In conclusion…" · "In summary…" · "Ultimately…"

### Group 8 — résumé and cover-letter specific (NG Career Strategy)
Noelle's example of a line that reads as generated end to end:
> "Results-driven professional with a proven track record of leveraging cross-functional collaboration to
> drive strategic growth initiatives."

Her second flag is the **statistically improbable quantified bullet** — a precise figure attached to an
abstract noun phrase with no named system, team or period:
> "Increased operational efficiency by 35% through strategic process optimization."

The tell is not the percentage. It is the missing referent.

## Sentence constructions to strike

**1. Negated contrast / contrast reframe.** The highest-value check in this file — named by three of six
sources, including as *the* most diagnostic move (Vollmer) and the only construction on Cook's otherwise
lexical 2026 list.
Before: "It's not about the tools, it's about the judgment."
After: "The judgment matters more than the tools." Or cut it; the sentence usually asserts nothing.

**2. "Not only X, but also Y."** (Gregory Agency) Split into two sentences or drop one half.

**3. Tricolon / rule-of-three fragment stack.** (Vollmer, quoting "Fast. Simple. Effective.")
Before: "Fast. Simple. Effective." After: "It runs in about four seconds and needs no configuration."
Concrete factual enumerations are not this — but more than two or three in one short document is a pattern.

**4. Staccato three-to-four-word sentences in a run.** Gregory Agency identifies this as the *newer* pattern
that replaced the older long-strung-together tell. Present-generation models default to it.

**5. Additive em dashes.** The precise version of the em-dash claim: the tell is the *function*, not the mark.
Dashes used additively, to append a qualifying clause, rather than to interrupt.
Before: "We shipped the migration — a process that took nine weeks — and moved on."
After: "We shipped the migration, which took nine weeks, and moved on." Reserve the dash for a real break in voice.

**6. Identical opening verbs across every bullet.** (NG Career Strategy) "Led… / Led… / Led…" Vary by what
actually happened: "Rewrote…", "Argued for…", "Inherited and fixed…".

**7. Job-description mirroring.** (NG Career Strategy) Dense skill lists reproducing the posting's phrasing
word for word — checkable by diffing the application against the ad. Note the tension with ATS term-matching:
match the vocabulary, do not clone the sentences.

## Formatting tells

- Bold-stemmed bullet lists — every bullet opening with a bolded two-or-three-word label plus colon (Vollmer).
- Unicode emoji in headings (Vollmer).
- Uniform bullet lengths / visually rectangular blocks (NG Career Strategy).
- Rhetorical colons in short form — two-word fragment, colon, payoff (Gregory Agency).
- Keyword repetition — the same term restated across several sections (NG Career Strategy).
- Cross-document tone inconsistency — résumé vs LinkedIn vs email. Recruiters lean on this comparison more
  than on any single word (NG Career Strategy).
- **The em dash alone is NOT a tell.** Gregory Agency rejects the claim directly; the mark is ordinary in
  edited prose. Use construction #5 instead of counting dashes. Counting will produce false positives against
  good human writing — and note the second-order problem: writers now avoid dashes defensively, so
  dash-*absence* is becoming its own signal. Do not drive the count to zero either.

## What changed / notable in 2026

1. **The register moved from ornate to plain.** The 2023–24 list (delve, tapestry, pivotal, underscore,
   landscape, foster, testament, robust, seamless, leverage) is Latinate and adjective-heavy. Cook's May 2026
   list is almost entirely short, plain, monosyllabic English. Different failure mode: not inflated formality
   but a borrowed *voice* — the cadence of an earnest LinkedIn thought-leadership post. Checking only for
   "delve" and "tapestry" now misses most current output. The older words remain elevated (Matsui) but are the
   most heavily prompted-against, so their absence proves nothing.
2. **Long sentences → short sentences.** Gregory Agency documents the flip. Advice written before ~2025 saying
   "AI writes long, over-subordinated sentences" is now backwards.
3. **The em dash has been actively devalued as a tell**, from two directions — never reliable (Gregory Agency),
   and narrowed to a functional rather than frequency test (Vollmer).
4. **"It's not X, it's Y" is the most durable tell on record.** On the 2023–24 lists and still independently
   named in 2026 by a creative-writing professor, Forbes, and a comms agency. Unlike vocabulary it has survived
   three years of prompt engineering against it. **Constructions have a much longer half-life than words.**
5. **Explicit warning from the editing profession that word lists expire.** Emily Primeaux, presenting at
   ACES 2026, expects nearly all specific tells to be different a year later; her stable indicators are
   structural — repetition, lack of clarity, and ideas that circle without landing. The third is the most
   checkable: a paragraph that restates its opening claim in different words and adds nothing.
6. **The recruiting side has largely stopped relying on language.** Noelle (Feb 2026) reports detection through
   *inconsistency* (résumé tone vs LinkedIn tone) and *statistical improbability* (unsupported precise metrics),
   and that the real filter has moved to behavioural interview questions where a candidate accounts for a bullet
   out loud. **For a job application the highest-risk tell in 2026 is a quantified claim you cannot narrate.**
   Every number on a résumé needs a story its owner can tell out loud.
7. **Quantitative confirmation now exists for the older layer.** Matsui (peer-reviewed) beats blog listicles
   when a claim needs to hold up, and surfaces four terms missing from most popular lists: boast, commendable,
   surpass, primarily.
